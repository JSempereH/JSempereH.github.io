This is the first post in a series related to Probabilistic InSAR. The end goal is to treat radar interferometry as a statistical estimation problem: given a noisy, wrapped, spatially correlated set of observations, recover the ground displacement signal we actually care about. Before any of that makes sense, we need to be precise about what a radar interferometer measures in the first place. That is the only goal of this post.

SAR: a radar camera, not an optical camera

A SAR (Synthetic Aperture Radar) satellite doesn’t take a passive picture of reflected light like an optical camera. It emits microwave pulses toward the ground and measures the signal that bounces back (backscatter). For each pulse, it recovers two things per ground point:

  • Amplitude: how much energy came back (related to roughness and material of the object).
  • Phase: where in the wave cycle the returning signal arrived, a value in $[0, 2\pi)$.

The phase is what interests us, because it encodes the distance traveled by the wave, with a precision of fractions of a wavelength (millimeters), far finer than the pixel’s spatial resolution (meters).

From a single image to an interferogram

A single SAR image is useless for measuring absolute distance: the phase is ambiguous modulo $2\pi$, and we don’t know how many full wave cycles fit along the path. The solution is differential: two acquisitions of the same area are taken at different times (or from slightly different positions), and the phase of one is subtracted from the other, pixel by pixel. This subtraction is called an interferogram.

If nothing changed between the two acquisitions, the differential phase should be essentially constant (aside from orbital geometry effects). If the ground moved in the satellite’s viewing direction (line of sight, LOS) between acquisitions, that motion is encoded in the differential phase.

What’s actually inside the interferometric phase

The phase observed at a pixel in an interferogram isn’t just “deformation.” It’s a mixture of several distinct physical contributions, all superimposed into the same real number modulo $2\pi$:

$$ \phi = \phi_{\text{topo}} + \phi_{\text{defo}} + \phi_{\text{atm}} + \phi_{\text{orb}} + \phi_{\text{noise}} $$
  • $\phi_{\text{topo}}$: the topographic effect (the height difference between the actual surface and a reference digital elevation model).
  • $\phi_{\text{defo}}$: the term we actually want, ground motion projected onto the line of sight.
  • $\phi_{\text{atm}}$: signal delay from propagating through the atmosphere (mainly water vapor), which varies in time and has nothing to do with the ground.
  • $\phi_{\text{orb}}$: residual errors in the satellite’s known orbit.
  • $\phi_{\text{noise}}$: instrument thermal noise and decorrelation (the target physically changed between acquisitions, so the phase is no longer comparable).

Separating these terms is, in essence, the whole problem of InSAR. You can already see why it’s ill-posed: you have one observed number per pixel and five physical processes mixed inside it. There’s no unique solution without imposing additional assumptions on how each term behaves (e.g., that deformation is “smooth” in time, or that the atmosphere varies spatially in a certain way).

A closer look: the topographic term

$\phi_{\text{topo}}$ is worth writing out explicitly, because unlike the others it has a clean closed form and it’s the easiest one to simulate. From the acquisition geometry sketched above, an elevation $h$ above the reference surface shortens the slant range by approximately $h \cos\theta$, where $\theta$ is the incidence angle. Projecting that onto the baseline gives the standard first-order result

$$ \phi_{\text{topo}} \approx \frac{4\pi}{\lambda} \frac{B_{\perp}}{R \sin\theta}\, h, $$

where $B_{\perp}$ is the component of the baseline perpendicular to the line of sight, $\lambda$ the radar wavelength, and $R$ the slant range. Because this is only ever observed modulo $2\pi$, there’s a characteristic elevation change that drives it through one full cycle, the ambiguity height:

$$ h_{2\pi} = \frac{\lambda R \sin\theta}{2 B_{\perp}}. $$

For Sentinel-1 ($\lambda = 5.55\,\text{cm}$, $R \approx 850\,\text{km}$, $\theta \approx 35°$) and a modest $B_{\perp} = 100\,\text{m}$ baseline, that comes out to roughly 135 m per fringe. We can simulate this directly: generate a synthetic DEM, apply the formula above, and wrap the result into $(-\pi, \pi]$ with np.angle(np.exp(i * phase)), exactly what a real interferogram looks like before unwrapping.

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)

# a synthetic "volcano" DEM
n = 300
x = np.linspace(-5000, 5000, n)  # metres
y = np.linspace(-5000, 5000, n)
X, Y = np.meshgrid(x, y)
r = np.sqrt(X**2 + Y**2)

cone = 1200 * np.exp(-(r / 2000) ** 1.5)
ripples = 40 * np.sin(r / 250) * np.exp(-r / 4000)
dem = cone + ripples

# Sentinel-1-like acquisition geometry
wavelength = 0.0555     # m, C-band
R = 850_000              # m, slant range
theta = np.deg2rad(35)   # incidence angle
B_perp = 100              # m, perpendicular baseline

k = 4 * np.pi * B_perp / (wavelength * R * np.sin(theta))
phase_topo = k * dem

h_amb = 2 * np.pi / k
print(f"ambiguity height: {h_amb:.1f} m")

noise = rng.normal(0, 0.15, dem.shape)  # decorrelation-like phase noise
wrapped = np.angle(np.exp(1j * (phase_topo + noise)))

ambiguity height: 135.3 m

Each closed ring around the peak is one full $2\pi$ cycle, i.e. one $h_{2\pi} \approx 135\,\text{m}$ step in elevation. A 1200 m cone produces roughly nine rings, which is exactly what’s in the figure. This is a good visual reminder of the next problem: the color at any pixel only tells you the phase modulo $2\pi$, not which ring, and therefore which elevation, you’re actually looking at.

The phase unwrapping problem

Since phase is only observed modulo $2\pi$, reconstructing the true signal requires deciding how many whole $2\pi$ cycles to add or subtract at each pixel to “unwrap” it into a continuous function. This is called phase unwrapping, and it’s itself a nontrivial combinatorial optimization problem, especially in areas with high noise or decorrelation. Unwrapping errors leave a characteristic fingerprint: jumps of exactly $\pm\lambda/2$ (half a wavelength) in the time series.

From a pair of images to a time series

A single interferogram gives a map of relative deformation between two dates. What’s actually interesting is the kinematic behavior: how a point moves over months or years. For that, a stack of $N$ SAR images of the same area is assembled (same satellite, similar geometry), and a phase time series per pixel is built, typically relative to a master image and a fixed reference point (which is assumed, with caveats, to be motionless).

This already starts to sound like statistics: for every point in space we have a vector of observations over time, with noise, from which we want to estimate a handful of kinematic parameters (velocity, whether there are jumps, whether there’s seasonality…). Formally, we can think of the time series at a point as a random vector $y \in \mathbb{R}^m$ with

$$ \mathbb{E}[y] = Ax, \qquad \operatorname{Cov}(y) = Q_{yy}, $$

where $A$ is a known design matrix, $x$ is an unknown parameter vector, and $Q_{yy}$ is the covariance matrix of the observations. This is nothing more than a generalized linear regression model. We’ll build on it once we get to the statistical modeling.

Persistent Scatterers (PS) and Distributed Scatterers (DS)

Not all pixels are equally useful. A SAR pixel spans several meters on a side and contains the combined contribution of many tiny physical “scatterers” (a rooftop, a rock, an antenna…). If those scatterers change configuration between acquisitions (moving vegetation, water, disturbed soil), the phase stops being comparable over time: this is temporal decorrelation, and it makes the pixel useless for time-series analysis.

Two classical strategies address this:

  • Persistent Scatterers (PS): pixels dominated by a single stable, highly reflective object (metallic structures, building corners, exposed rock). Their phase is reliable over time because the “object” doesn’t change.
  • Distributed Scatterers (DS): groups of spatially homogeneous pixels (natural terrain, fields) that, when averaged, yield a more stable phase signal, even though individually they’re noisy.

With this vocabulary in place (interferogram, differential phase, its components, unwrapping, PS/DS, time series), we have what’s needed to move on to the underlying statistical model: how a point’s time series is formalized as a Gaussian random vector and estimated via generalized least squares.
