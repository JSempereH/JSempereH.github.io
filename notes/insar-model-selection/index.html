This is the third post in the Probabilistic InSAR series. The previous post set up the functional-stochastic model for a point’s phase time series and showed why the residual of a generalized least squares fit follows a chi-squared distribution. Here we use that result to compare several candidate deformation models against each other, not just against pure noise.

The setup: one null model, many alternatives

At every point, we start from a baseline assumption: steady-state motion, a single column in the design matrix,

$$ H_0: \quad \mathbb{E}[y] = Ax, \qquad \operatorname{Cov}(y) = Q_{yy}. $$

This is the null hypothesis. But we suspect the point might actually be doing something richer: seasonal oscillation, a discrete jump, an exponential relaxation. Each of these possibilities is encoded as an extra set of columns $C_j$ added to the model, with an associated extra parameter vector $\nabla_j$ of dimension $q$:

$$ H_j: \quad \mathbb{E}[y] = Ax + C_j \nabla_j, \qquad \nabla_j \neq 0. $$

$H_j$ is not one fixed alternative but a family: one per candidate extra term (or combination of terms) we’re willing to test. The question is no longer “is $H_0$ true or false,” but “among $H_0$ and a whole library of $H_j$’s, which one best explains the data.”

Recap: the test statistic and its distribution

From the previous post, the key quantity for testing $H_0$ against a specific $H_j$ is a residual-based quadratic form. Its general version is

$$ T_q^j = \hat e_0^T \, Q_{yy}^{-1} C_j \left(C_j^T Q_{yy}^{-1} Q_{\hat e_0 \hat e_0} Q_{yy}^{-1} C_j\right)^{-1} C_j^T Q_{yy}^{-1} \, \hat e_0, $$

where $\hat e_0$ is the residual computed under $H_0$ (we never need to actually fit $H_j$; everything is expressed in terms of the null-hypothesis residual and matrices that depend only on $A$, $C_j$, and $Q_{yy}$). Under $H_0$, this statistic follows a central chi-squared distribution with $q$ degrees of freedom. Under $H_j$, it follows a noncentral chi-squared with the same $q$ degrees of freedom and noncentrality parameter

$$ \lambda_j = \nabla_j^T C_j^T Q_{yy}^{-1} Q_{\hat e_0 \hat e_0} Q_{yy}^{-1} C_j \nabla_j, $$

which, following the previous post’s derivation, is literally the squared size of the part of $C_j \nabla_j$ that survives projection onto the residual space. A large true effect gives a large $\lambda_j$, which pushes the noncentral chi-squared away from zero and makes rejection more likely: this is exactly the statistical notion of test power.

Why we can’t just compare test statistics directly

Different alternative hypotheses can have different dimensions $q$. A seasonal model might add $q=2$ parameters (sine and cosine coefficients); a single jump adds $q=1$; two jumps add $q=2$; and so on. A chi-squared statistic with more degrees of freedom is, all else equal, larger on average just by construction, since the mean of a central $\chi^2_q$ is $q$ itself. Comparing $T_q^j$ across models with different $q$ directly would systematically favor richer models for no good statistical reason, similar to why raw $R^2$ in ordinary regression always improves as you add predictors, regardless of whether they’re meaningful.

We need a way to put statistics with different degrees of freedom on a common footing before comparing them.

Test ratios: normalizing by the critical value

The natural fix is to divide each test statistic by the threshold that would make it “just significant” at some chosen significance level $\alpha_j$, i.e. by the critical value $\chi^2_{\alpha_j}(q_j)$ of the corresponding central chi-squared distribution:

$$ \underline{T}_{q_j}^j = T_{q_j}^j \big/ \chi^2_{\alpha_j}(q_j). $$

This is called a test ratio. Its interpretation is clean: if $\underline T_{q_j}^j > 1$, the observed statistic exceeds what would be needed to reject $H_0$ at level $\alpha_j$, so $H_j$ is favored over $H_0$. If $\underline T_{q_j}^j \le 1$, $H_j$ is not supported by the data any more than $H_0$ is, and gets discarded. Among all alternatives that clear this bar, the one with the largest test ratio is treated as the most probable model:

$$ \underline T_{q_B}^B = \max_j \left\{ \underline T_{q_j}^j \right\}. $$

This still leaves an open question, though: how should $\alpha_j$ be chosen for each candidate model? If we just picked the same $\alpha$ for every $j$ regardless of $q_j$, we would again be comparing models unfairly, because the same $\alpha$ corresponds to different amounts of statistical stringency depending on the dimension of the test.

Fixing power instead of significance

This is where a classical idea from geodetic testing theory, due to Baarda, comes in: instead of fixing $\alpha_j$ directly, fix the power of every test, i.e. the probability of correctly rejecting $H_0$ when $H_j$ is in fact true, and let $\alpha_j$ adjust itself for each dimension $q_j$ to match that fixed power.

Concretely, pick a reference significance level $\alpha_0$ and a reference power $\gamma_0$ for a test with one degree of freedom ($q=1$). This pair determines a noncentrality parameter $\lambda_0$ through the relationship between significance, power, and noncentrality for a chi-squared test:

$$ \lambda_0 = \lambda(\gamma_0, \alpha_0, q=1). $$

Now, for an alternative hypothesis with $q_j$ degrees of freedom instead of $1$, we ask: what significance level $\alpha_j$ would keep the power equal to that same $\gamma_0$, given the noncentrality parameter that corresponds to $q_j$ degrees of freedom? Solving that relationship for $\alpha_j$ gives exactly the significance level to plug into the test ratio above.

In practice, Chang & Hanssen (2016) fix $\alpha_0 = 1/(2m)$, tying the reference significance level to how many epochs $m$ the time series actually has, and $\gamma_0 = 50\%$, the power at which a “just as detectable” effect is exactly as likely to be caught as missed.

The effect is subtle but important: instead of every test having the same probability of a false positive (fixed $\alpha$), every test now has the same probability of correctly detecting a “just as detectable” true effect (fixed $\gamma_0$), regardless of how many parameters that effect involves. A two-parameter seasonal model and a one-parameter jump model are now judged on equal footing: both need equally “hard to miss” evidence, calibrated for their own dimension, before being preferred over the null.

A worked example

Suppose we have a single point observed at $m = 30$ epochs, and after fitting the steady-state null model $H_0$ (linear velocity only, $n=1$), we compute the overall test statistic and find it exceeds its critical value: $H_0$ is rejected. There’s some structure in the residual $\hat e_0$ that a constant velocity doesn’t explain, and we now need to figure out what.

Say we have two candidate alternatives in our library:

  • $H_1$: linear velocity plus a seasonal term (columns $\sin(2\pi t_i)$, $\cos(2\pi t_i)$), so $q_1 = 2$.
  • $H_2$: linear velocity plus a single discrete jump at some known epoch, so $q_2 = 1$.

We compute both test statistics directly from $\hat e_0$ (no need to refit anything), and suppose the results are

$$ T_2^1 = 9.8, \qquad T_1^2 = 7.1. $$

At face value, $H_1$’s statistic is larger. But $H_1$ has two degrees of freedom against $H_2$’s one, so a naive comparison is unfair: a $\chi^2_2$ statistic tends to run higher than a $\chi^2_1$ statistic even under pure noise, simply because it’s summing two squared terms instead of one.

This is exactly what the power-calibrated significance levels are for. Suppose, from the power-equalization step, we obtain $\alpha_1 \approx 0.10$ for the two-parameter test and $\alpha_2 \approx 0.025$ for the one-parameter test (a smaller $\alpha$ for fewer degrees of freedom, in this illustration, is what the power-matching condition happens to produce here). Looking up the corresponding critical values of the central chi-squared distribution:

$$ \chi^2_{0.10}(2) \approx 4.61, \qquad \chi^2_{0.025}(1) \approx 5.02. $$

The test ratios become

$$ \underline T_2^1 = \frac{9.8}{4.61} \approx 2.13, \qquad \underline T_1^2 = \frac{7.1}{5.02} \approx 1.41. $$

Both ratios exceed 1, so both $H_1$ and $H_2$ are preferred over $H_0$. But once we’ve corrected for the difference in degrees of freedom, $H_1$ comes out clearly ahead: $2.13 > 1.41$. The seasonal model is selected as the best-supported explanation for this point, even though its raw test statistic wasn’t proportionally as far ahead of $H_2$’s as the uncorrected numbers suggested.

Why not just use AIC or BIC?

Information criteria like AIC and BIC solve a closely related problem: they penalize models with more parameters to avoid overfitting, trading off goodness of fit against model complexity. The B-method approach shares that spirit but reaches it differently. AIC and BIC compare models through a fixed penalty per parameter (2 per parameter for AIC, $\ln m$ per parameter for BIC), independent of the actual noise structure or how detectable an effect is in practice. The power-based approach instead asks a directly statistical question: for this specific type of effect, at this specific noise level, would the test have caught it reliably? The penalty for extra parameters emerges implicitly from requiring equal detection power, rather than being imposed as a fixed constant. Both approaches are, in the end, model selection procedures with a built-in complexity penalty, just derived from different first principles: information theory for AIC/BIC, hypothesis testing theory here.

Putting it together: the testing sequence

This whole procedure has a name in geodetic testing theory: Detection, Identification, Adaptation (DIA), due to Teunissen. For a single point, it looks like this:

  1. Detection. Test $H_0$ alone against the data, using the residual-based quadratic form from the previous post (this is sometimes called an overall model test). If the statistic doesn’t exceed its critical value, there’s no evidence the steady-state model is wrong, and we stop here.
  2. Identification. If $H_0$ is rejected, loop over the whole library of alternative hypotheses $H_j$, compute each $T_{q_j}^j$, convert it into a test ratio $\underline T_{q_j}^j$ using the power-calibrated $\alpha_j$, and discard any $H_j$ with a ratio at or below 1. Among the survivors, the one with the largest test ratio is the best-supported model.
  3. Adaptation. Re-estimate the parameters under the winning model, and check whether that estimate is actually good enough to trust.

Notice what this buys computationally: steps 1 and 2 only ever need the residual under $H_0$ and a handful of matrices that depend on $A$, $C_j$, and $Q_{yy}$, none of which depend on the actual data value $y$ beyond that single residual. This means the expensive part, comparing dozens or hundreds of candidate models, never requires explicitly refitting each candidate model to the data. That computational shortcut is what makes it feasible to repeat this whole procedure independently at every point in a scene with millions of them.

Adaptation: is the estimate actually good enough?

Step 3 is more than a formality. Picking the model with the best test ratio only says $H_j$ explains the residual better than $H_0$ does, it says nothing about whether the resulting parameter estimates are themselves precise and reliable enough to report. DIA’s adaptation step adds that check explicitly.

Once $H_j$ is selected, re-estimate the full parameter vector $\hat x = [\hat x^T, \hat\nabla^T]^T$ against the enlarged design matrix $[A \;\; C_j]$, with covariance

$$ Q_{\hat x \hat x} = \left([A \;\; C_j]^T Q_{yy}^{-1} [A \;\; C_j]\right)^{-1}. $$

This is judged against a criterion matrix $H_x$, a matrix encoding the maximum uncertainty considered acceptable for these parameters (for instance, the largest tolerable variance on an estimated velocity). Comparing the two via the generalized eigenvalue problem $Q_{\hat x \hat x} v = \zeta H_x v$ gives a largest eigenvalue $\zeta_{\max}$: the estimate is accepted only if $\zeta_{\max} \le 1$, i.e. if $Q_{\hat x \hat x}$ is nowhere larger than what the criterion allows.

This is the same power machinery from earlier in the post, run in reverse. Where detection and identification ask “is there a signal large enough to be worth modeling,” reliability asks “if a signal of a given size were present, would this particular setup (this $A$, $C_j$, $Q_{yy}$, at this point) have been able to catch it,” via

$$ \nabla^T C_j^T Q_{\hat e_0 \hat e_0}^{-1} C_j \nabla = \lambda(\gamma, \alpha, q). $$

A small $\nabla$ pushes this equality toward requiring a large $\lambda$, meaning low power, meaning the setup is unreliable for effects that size. This closes the loop: it’s not enough for a model to win the comparison against its rivals, the resulting estimate has to clear an absolute precision bar too, before it gets reported as the answer for that point.
