Ordinary least squares breaks down when $p \gg n$: the matrix $X^\top X$ becomes singular and $\hat{\beta} = (X^\top X)^{-1} X^\top Y$ does not exist. This post surveys four strategies for dealing with that problem. The first two (PCR and PLS) compress the predictors into a small number of components before regressing. The last two (Ridge and Lasso) keep all $p$ predictors but penalise large coefficients. They attack the same problem from opposite directions, and which one works best depends entirely on the structure of the signal in your data.

Notation

  • $Y \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times k}$: $n$ observations on $k$ response variables.
  • $X \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times p}$: $n$ observations on $p$ predictors.

Principal Components Regression

The simplest fix for rank-deficient $X$ is to replace the original $p$ predictors with $M \ll p$ orthogonal linear combinations that capture most of the variance in $X$. That is exactly what PCR does.

We apply the thin SVD to the column-centred $X$:

$$ X = U \Sigma V^\top, \qquad U^\top U = V^\top V = I_r $$

The columns of $V \in \mathbb{R}^{p \times r}$ are the loadings (directions in predictor space) and the columns of $Z = U\Sigma = XV \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times r}$ are the scores. PCR keeps the first $M$ scores — those corresponding to the $M$ largest singular values — and regresses $Y$ on them:

$$ \hat{Y}_{\text{PCR}} = Z_M \hat{B}_M, \qquad \hat{B}_M = (Z_M^\top Z_M)^{-1} Z_M^\top Y. $$

Because the columns of $Z_M$ are orthogonal, this reduces to $M$ independent univariate regressions, one per component.

The catch. PCA is blind to $Y$: it selects directions that explain the variance of $X$, with no regard for how useful those directions are for predicting $Y$. If the signal lives in a low-variance corner of the predictor space, PCR will miss it entirely unless $M$ is large enough to reach that corner, at which point we have gained little over OLS.

Partial Least Squares

PLS fixes the blind spot of PCR by letting $Y$ participate in the construction of the components. Instead of maximising the variance of $X$ explained by each component, PLS maximises the covariance between the component and $Y$. The result is a supervised dimensionality-reduction method: the latent directions are chosen precisely because they are predictive, not merely because they are large.

Reference: Abdi, H. (2010). Partial least squares regression and projection on latent structure regression (PLS Regression). WIREs Computational Statistics, 2(1), 97–106. https://doi.org/10.1002/wics.51

The NIPALS algorithm (univariate $y$)

Like PCR, PLS is not scale-invariant, so we first standardise each $x_j$ to mean $0$ and variance $1$. Set $\hat{y}^{(0)} = \bar{y}\,\mathbf{1}$ and $x_j^{(0)} = x_j$ for $j = 1, \dots, p$.

For $m = 1, 2, \dots, M$:

  1. Score: $z_m = \displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{p} \psi_{mj}\, x_j^{(m-1)}$, where the weights are $\psi_{mj} = \langle x_j^{(m-1)},\, y \rangle$.

  2. Regress $y$ on $z_m$:

    $$ \hat{\theta}_m = \frac{\langle z_m, y \rangle}{\langle z_m, z_m \rangle} $$

  3. Update the fitted values:

    $$ \hat{y}^{(m)} = \hat{y}^{(m-1)} + \hat{\theta}_m z_m $$

  4. Deflate — orthogonalise each predictor with respect to $z_m$:

    $$ x_j^{(m)} = x_j^{(m-1)} - \frac{\langle z_m,\, x_j^{(m-1)} \rangle} {\langle z_m, z_m \rangle}\, z_m, \qquad j = 1, \dots, p. $$

The output is the sequence $\bigl\{\hat{y}^{(m)}\bigr\}_{m=1}^{M}$. Because each $z_m$ is a linear combination of the (deflated) $x_j$’s, we always have $\hat{y}^{(m)} = X\hat{\beta}^{(m)}$ for a recoverable coefficient vector $\hat{\beta}^{(m)}$.

Intuition. The weight $\psi_{mj} = \langle x_j^{(m-1)}, y \rangle$ is the sample covariance between predictor $j$ and the response. Predictors that correlate more strongly with $y$ receive larger weights, so $z_m$ points in the direction of maximum covariance with $y$, not maximum variance of $X$ alone. Deflation then removes the variation already explained, so successive components are non-redundant.

Python example (PCR vs PLS)

The clearest way to see the difference between PCR and PLS is to construct a dataset where they are forced to diverge. We build $X$ with two kinds of columns: 2 signal columns (low variance, $\sigma = 1$) that directly determine $y$, and 10 noise columns (high variance, $\sigma = 10$) that are completely unrelated to $y$. PCR ranks directions by variance, so it focuses on the noise and wastes its early components on it. PLS ranks directions by covariance with $y$, so it homes in on the signal from the very first component.

Here is the Gist.

import numpy as np
from sklearn.cross_decomposition import PLSRegression
from sklearn.decomposition import PCA
from sklearn.linear_model import LinearRegression
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler
from sklearn.model_selection import cross_val_score
from sklearn.pipeline import Pipeline

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n = 100

signal = rng.standard_normal((n, 2))        # low variance, drives y
noise  = rng.standard_normal((n, 10)) * 10  # high variance, unrelated to y

y = 3 * signal[:, 0] - 2 * signal[:, 1] + 0.5 * rng.standard_normal(n)
X = np.hstack([signal, noise])

pcr_pipe = Pipeline([("scaler", StandardScaler()),
                     ("pca", PCA(n_components=2)),
                     ("ols", LinearRegression())])

pls_pipe = Pipeline([("scaler", StandardScaler()),
                     ("pls", PLSRegression(n_components=2))])

for M in [1, 2, 3, 5]:
    pcr_pipe.set_params(pca__n_components=M)
    pls_pipe.set_params(pls__n_components=M)

    pcr_cv = cross_val_score(pcr_pipe, X, y, cv=5, scoring="r2").mean()
    pls_cv = cross_val_score(pls_pipe, X, y, cv=5, scoring="r2").mean()

    print(f"M={M}  PCR CV-R² = {pcr_cv:.3f}   PLS CV-R² = {pls_cv:.3f}")

M=1  PCR CV-R² = 0.018   PLS CV-R² = 0.709
M=2  PCR CV-R² = 0.096   PLS CV-R² = 0.913
M=3  PCR CV-R² = 0.184   PLS CV-R² = 0.953
M=5  PCR CV-R² = 0.250   PLS CV-R² = 0.961

Why does this happen? PCR is blind to $Y$: its first components capture the high-variance noise directions. PLS computes weights $\psi_{mj} = \langle x_j, y \rangle$, so the noisy predictors, uncorrelated with $y$, get near-zero weight and are effectively ignored from the very first component.

Penalised Regression: Ridge and Lasso

PCR and PLS deal with multicollinearity by discarding dimensions. But there is a fundamentally different approach: keep all $p$ predictors and instead add a penalty term to the OLS objective that discourages large coefficients. This trades a potential bias in the estimates for a reduction in variance — the classic bias–variance trade-off — and it sidesteps the need to choose how many components to retain.

Ridge Regression

Ridge adds an $\ell_2$ penalty on the coefficient vector:

$$ \hat{\beta}^{\text{ridge}} = \arg\min_{\beta} \left\{ \|y - X\beta\|^2 + \lambda \|\beta\|_2^2 \right\} $$

The closed-form solution is

$$ \hat{\beta}^{\text{ridge}} = (X^\top X + \lambda I)^{-1} X^\top y. $$

Adding $\lambda I$ makes the matrix positive definite even when $X^\top X$ is singular, so Ridge is always well-defined. As $\lambda$ grows, all coefficients are shrunk continuously towards zero, but none is ever set exactly to zero. Ridge is therefore the natural choice when you believe most predictors carry a weak but genuine signal.

Lasso Regression

Lasso replaces the squared penalty with an $\ell_1$ penalty:

$$ \hat{\beta}^{\text{lasso}} = \arg\min_{\beta} \left\{ \|y - X\beta\|^2 + \lambda \|\beta\|_1 \right\} $$

where $\|\beta\|_1 = \sum_j |\beta_j|$. There is no closed form; the problem is solved efficiently by coordinate descent. The crucial difference from Ridge is that the $\ell_1$ penalty has corners at zero: for large enough $\lambda$ many coefficients are driven to exactly zero. Lasso therefore performs automatic variable selection, it shrinks and selects simultaneously.

Geometric intuition

The contrast is sharpest in the constrained formulation. Both methods minimise the RSS subject to a budget on $\|\beta\|$:

  • Ridge: $\|\beta\|_2^2 \leq t$, the feasible set is a smooth sphere.
  • Lasso: $\|\beta\|_1 \leq t$, the feasible set is a diamond with corners on the coordinate axes.

The RSS contours (ellipses centred at the OLS estimate) are most likely to first touch the diamond at a corner, where one or more $\beta_j = 0$. That geometric accident is why Lasso produces sparse solutions while Ridge does not.

Python example (Ridge vs Lasso)

import numpy as np
from sklearn.linear_model import RidgeCV, LassoCV
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n, p = 100, 150
X = rng.standard_normal((n, p))

# Only the first 5 predictors truly matter
true_beta = np.zeros(p)
true_beta[:5] = [3, -2, 1.5, -1, 2]
y = X @ true_beta + rng.standard_normal(n)

X_s = StandardScaler().fit_transform(X)

ridge_cv = RidgeCV(alphas=np.logspace(-3, 3, 100), cv=5).fit(X_s, y)
print(f"Ridge  best λ={ridge_cv.alpha_:.4f}  "
      f"non-zero coefs: {np.sum(ridge_cv.coef_ != 0)}")

lasso_cv = LassoCV(cv=5, random_state=0).fit(X_s, y)
n_nonzero = np.sum(lasso_cv.coef_ != 0)
print(f"Lasso  best λ={lasso_cv.alpha_:.4f}  "
      f"non-zero coefs: {n_nonzero}")

Ridge  best λ=53.3670  non-zero coefs: 150
Lasso  best λ=0.1263  non-zero coefs: 26

Ridge keeps all 150 coefficients non-zero; Lasso correctly zeroes out most of them, leaving only a handful near the five true predictors. Let’s visualise:

import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(12, 4), sharey=True)

for ax, coef, title in zip(
    axes,
    [ridge_cv.coef_, lasso_cv.coef_],
    ["Ridge (all 150 non-zero)", f"Lasso ({n_nonzero} non-zero)"],
):
    colors = ["steelblue" if c != 0 else "lightgrey" for c in coef]
    ax.bar(range(p), coef, color=colors)
    ax.axhline(0, color="black", linewidth=0.8)
    ax.set_title(title)
    ax.set_xlabel("Predictor index")

axes[0].set_ylabel("Coefficient value")
for ax in axes:
    for i in range(5):
        ax.axvline(i, color="red", linewidth=1, linestyle="--", alpha=0.5)

plt.suptitle("True predictors marked in red", fontsize=11)
plt.tight_layout()

Elastic Net: the best of both

When predictors are strongly correlated, Lasso tends to pick one arbitrarily and discard the rest, an unstable outcome. Elastic Net combines both penalties to avoid this:

$$ \hat{\beta}^{\text{EN}} = \arg\min_{\beta} \left\{ \|y - X\beta\|^2 + \lambda_1 \|\beta\|_1 + \lambda_2 \|\beta\|_2^2 \right\} $$

It inherits sparsity from the $\ell_1$ term and the grouping effect (correlated predictors shrink together) from the $\ell_2$ term. In practice it is often the safest default when you suspect a sparse signal but are unsure how correlated the predictors are.

from sklearn.linear_model import ElasticNetCV

enet_cv = ElasticNetCV(cv=5, random_state=0).fit(X_s, y)
n_nonzero = np.sum(enet_cv.coef_ != 0)
print(f"ElasticNet  best λ={enet_cv.alpha_:.4f}  "
      f"l1_ratio={enet_cv.l1_ratio_:.2f}  non-zero coefs: {n_nonzero}")

ElasticNet  best λ=0.1445  l1_ratio=0.50  non-zero coefs: 51

When to use each method

All four methods solve the same problem, but they make different assumptions about where the signal lives.

  • Dense signal ($p \gg n$, many weak predictors) → Ridge
  • Sparse signal ($p \gg n$, few true predictors) → Lasso or Elastic Net
  • Correlated predictors, sparse signal → Elastic Net
  • Latent structure matters for interpretability → PLS
  • Unsupervised compression of $X$ then regression → PCR

If you are unsure, cross-validate all of them on a held-out set, the differences in practice can be huge, as the PCR vs PLS example above shows.
