This is the second post in the Probabilistic InSAR series. The previous post worked out what the interferometric phase actually contains, how it wraps, and how a stack of interferograms becomes a phase time series per pixel. Here we take that time series and turn it into an honest statistical estimation problem.

From a phase time series to a regression problem

Take a single point in space, one persistent scatterer, and look at its phase time series across $m$ SAR acquisitions, already converted to a deformation-equivalent quantity (millimeters, say) and referenced to a master acquisition and a reference point. Call this vector of observations $y \in \mathbb{R}^m$.

Note. Constructing $y$ this way is not as clean as it sounds, because “referenced to a reference point” means every point’s series inherits whatever noise or instability that single reference point has. Chang & Hanssen (2016) correct for this with a reference-point-noise (RPN) reduction step, run once before any of the modeling below: fit and remove a linear trend from every point’s raw series, average the leftover residual across all points epoch by epoch, and subtract that shared average from every series. This isolates the noise common to all points (coming from the reference itself) from the noise genuinely specific to each point, which is the only kind the model in the rest of this post is meant to explain.

The working assumption is that $y$ is a realization of a Gaussian random vector:

$$ \mathbb{E}[y] = Ax, \qquad \operatorname{Cov}(y) = Q_{yy} = \sigma^2 R_{yy}, $$

where:

  • $A \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}$ is a design matrix, known and fixed. It encodes which kinematic behaviors we allow the point to exhibit.
  • $x \in \mathbb{R}^n$ is the unknown parameter vector: the actual physical quantities we want to estimate (a velocity, an amplitude, an offset…).
  • $Q_{yy}$ is the covariance matrix of the observations, split into a scalar variance factor $\sigma^2$ and a cofactor matrix $R_{yy}$ that encodes the relative precision and correlation structure between epochs.

This is nothing more than a linear regression model with a general, possibly non-diagonal covariance matrix, the same object you’d meet in generalized least squares (GLS), just wearing geodetic notation. The “generalized” part matters: SAR observations at different epochs are not necessarily independent or equally precise, so assuming $Q_{yy} = \sigma^2 I$ is a simplification, not a law of nature.

What goes into the design matrix $A$

The power of this formulation is that $A$ is a modeling choice, not something derived from the data. Each column of $A$ is a candidate temporal shape the deformation signal might follow, evaluated at the $m$ acquisition times $t_1, \dots, t_m$. For instance:

  • Steady, linear motion: a column equal to the temporal baseline itself, $t_i$, so that the associated parameter is a velocity in mm/year.
  • Seasonal motion: a pair of columns $\sin(2\pi t_i)$, $\cos(2\pi t_i)$, so the associated parameters combine into an amplitude and phase offset of an annual cycle.
  • A discrete jump: a column that is $0$ before some epoch $t_k$ and $1$ from $t_k$ onward (a Heaviside step), so the parameter is the size of the jump.

Stacking columns builds increasingly rich models. A point with only a linear-velocity column has $n = 1$; a point with linear velocity plus a seasonal term has $n = 3$, and so on. The choice of which columns to include is exactly the choice of which physical process we believe is happening at that point, and different points can, in principle, warrant different $A$ matrices.

Estimating $x$: generalized least squares

Given $A$ and $Q_{yy}$, the best linear unbiased estimator of $x$ (under the Gaussian assumption, also the maximum-likelihood estimator) is the familiar GLS solution:

$$ \hat{x} = \left(A^T Q_{yy}^{-1} A\right)^{-1} A^T Q_{yy}^{-1} y. $$

This minimizes the weighted sum of squared residuals, where the weighting is given by $Q_{yy}^{-1}$: epochs with larger variance, or strong correlations with other epochs, contribute less “independent” information to the estimate.

From $\hat{x}$ we get fitted values $\hat{y} = A\hat{x}$ and residuals

$$ \hat{e} = y - \hat{y} = y - A\hat{x}. $$

The residual vector $\hat{e}$ is what’s left over after the assumed model has explained everything it can. Its covariance matrix is not $Q_{yy}$ itself, but a related, smaller quantity:

$$ Q_{\hat{e}\hat{e}} = Q_{yy} - A\left(A^T Q_{yy}^{-1} A\right)^{-1} A^T. $$

This is a standard fact from linear model theory: projecting the data onto the column space of $A$ removes some degrees of freedom from the residual, so $Q_{\hat e \hat e}$ is “smaller” than $Q_{yy}$ in a precise sense (it’s singular, of rank $m - n$).

Why the residual matters more than the fit

At first glance, the natural question is “how good is $\hat x$?” But for the purposes of choosing between different candidate models, different design matrices $A$, the more useful object turns out to be the residual $\hat e$ itself, not the parameter estimate.

Intuitively: if the assumed model $A$ is correct, $\hat e$ should look like pure noise, consistent with $Q_{\hat e \hat e}$. If the model is missing something (an unmodeled jump, an unmodeled seasonal cycle), that missing structure will leak into $\hat e$ as a systematic pattern rather than pure noise. A quadratic form of the residual against its own covariance,

$$ \hat{e}^T Q_{yy}^{-1} \hat{e}, $$

turns this intuition into a number: under the assumed model and Gaussian noise, this quantity follows a central chi-squared distribution with $m - n$ degrees of freedom. If the true process includes something the model doesn’t capture, the same quantity follows a noncentral chi-squared distribution instead, pulled away from zero by whatever structure was left unmodeled.

Note. If we multiply everything by $Q_{yy}^{-1/2}$, the inverse square root of the covariance matrix (this is called whitening), the transformed vector $z = Q_{yy}^{-1/2} y$ has covariance exactly $I$, its entries are uncorrelated and all have variance 1, instead of the correlated, unequal-variance noise in $y$.

Writing $\tilde A = Q_{yy}^{-1/2}A$, distribute the $Q_{yy}^{-1/2}$ factors inside the GLS formula. In $A^TQ_{yy}^{-1}A$, the middle collapses as $Q_{yy}^{1/2}Q_{yy}^{-1}Q_{yy}^{1/2} = Q_{yy}^{1/2}Q_{yy}^{-1/2}Q_{yy}^{-1/2}Q_{yy}^{1/2} = I$ ($Q_{yy}^{1/2}$ and $Q_{yy}^{-1/2}$ are functions of the same matrix, so they commute and cancel), leaving $A^TQ_{yy}^{-1}A = \tilde A^T\tilde A$ and, by the same cancellation, $A^TQ_{yy}^{-1}y = \tilde A^Tz$. Substituting,

$$\hat x = (\tilde A^T\tilde A)^{-1}\tilde A^Tz,$$

which is exactly the ordinary least-squares estimator of $z$ on $\tilde A$: GLS on the correlated data $y$ is just OLS on the whitened data $z$, with $\tilde A$ playing the role of the design matrix. Now multiply the residual definition $\hat e = y - A\hat x$ by $Q_{yy}^{-1/2}$ and substitute this $\hat x$ in:

$$ > Q_{yy}^{-1/2}\hat e = z - \tilde A\hat x > = z - \tilde A(\tilde A^T\tilde A)^{-1}\tilde A^Tz = (I - \tilde P)z, > $$

where $\tilde P := \tilde A(\tilde A^T\tilde A)^{-1}\tilde A^T$. Squaring both sides, and using that $I - \tilde P$ is symmetric and idempotent, gives $\hat e^T Q_{yy}^{-1}\hat e = z^T(I - \tilde P)z$, a rank-$(m-n)$ projector applied to $z$.

Any such projector splits a covariance-$I$ Gaussian vector into $m - n$ independent, unit-variance coordinates, and a sum of $k$ squared independent unit-variance Gaussians is, by definition, $\chi^2_k$ (noncentral if their means aren’t zero).

This gives us a way to ask whether a residual looks too large to be explained by noise alone, and, more ambitiously, which among several candidate extra terms best explains why it’s too large.

That question, comparing several candidate models against each other and not just against pure noise, is where the working machinery of hypothesis testing over a whole library of design matrices comes in.
