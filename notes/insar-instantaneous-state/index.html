The previous post showed how to fold a new observation into an estimate without reprocessing the whole history, as long as the underlying parameter $x$ stays exactly time-invariant. That’s still the limiting assumption. This post lifts it: instead of a fixed velocity, we let the instantaneous velocity itself be a random process that is allowed to wander, smoothly, over time. Wang et al. (2026) call the resulting state vector, position plus instantaneous velocity, the instantaneous state (IS).

Why velocity, not just position, is part of the state

The natural first instinct is to track only position $P_t$ and let velocity be whatever falls out of differentiating it. Wang et al. treat velocity $v_t$ as its own, independently estimated state component instead. The reason is that position alone gives you no way to express a belief about how quickly things are allowed to change; velocity is where that belief actually lives. Making it explicit means we can attach a smoothness constraint directly to velocity, and let position inherit smooth behavior as a consequence, rather than trying to constrain position directly.

The state (dropping the static nuisance parameters $\Delta H$, $\eta$ for now, since they don’t participate in the dynamics) is $x_t = [P_t, v_t]^T$, and the model for how it evolves is not “constant velocity” but “velocity that drifts, with memory.”

The Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process

Model $v_t$ as the solution of a stochastic differential equation:

$$ \dot v_t = -\rho\, v_t + z(t), \qquad \rho = 1/\tau > 0, $$

where $z(t)$ is white noise. This is the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck (OU) process: a zero-mean, mean-reverting random walk in velocity. Without the $-\rho v_t$ term this would just be a random walk (velocity drifts unboundedly). The extra term constantly pulls $v_t$ back toward zero, at a rate set by $\rho$, so that fluctuations in velocity have a finite memory instead of accumulating forever. That memory is exactly quantified by the autocovariance function

$$ C_v(\Delta t) = \sigma_v^2\, e^{-\Delta t/\tau}, $$

two velocities $\Delta t$ apart are correlated, with the correlation decaying exponentially, and $\tau$ is the time lag at which that correlation has dropped to $e^{-1}\approx 0.37$.

Note: $\sigma_v$ and $\tau$ are not estimated from the data, they’re a prior on how the object is expected to behave, chosen from context (the kind of structure, the physical process suspected) before looking at the time series. Choosing them is exactly analogous to choosing which columns go into $A$ back in post II, it’s a modeling decision, not a fit.

From the SDE to a discrete transition matrix

To use this inside a recursive estimator, we need the discrete-time equivalent of the OU process: given $x_{t-1} = [P_{t-1}, v_{t-1}]^T$, what’s the exact distribution of $x_t$ one step later? Stack position and velocity into a linear system:

$$ \dot x_t = F x_t + G z_t, \qquad F = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & -\rho \end{bmatrix}, \qquad G = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}. $$

The noise-free part of the solution is the matrix exponential $\Phi_{t,t-1} = e^{F\Delta t}$, and it can be computed by hand. $F$ has eigenvalues $0$ and $-\rho$ (its characteristic polynomial is $\lambda^2+\rho\lambda = \lambda(\lambda+\rho)$), with eigenvectors $(1,0)^T$ and $(1,-\rho)^T$ respectively. Diagonalizing $F = V D V^{-1}$ with $V = \begin{bmatrix}1&1\\0&-\rho\end{bmatrix}$, $D=\operatorname{diag}(0,-\rho)$, and $V^{-1} = \begin{bmatrix}1&1/\rho\\0&-1/\rho\end{bmatrix}$, gives

$$ e^{F\Delta t} = V\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\0&e^{-\rho\Delta t}\end{bmatrix}V^{-1} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & \dfrac{1}{\rho}\left(1-e^{-\rho\Delta t}\right) \\[4pt] 0 & e^{-\rho\Delta t} \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & \tau\left(1-e^{-\Delta t/\tau}\right) \\[4pt] 0 & e^{-\Delta t/\tau} \end{bmatrix}, $$

using $\tau = 1/\rho$. This is exactly $\Phi_{t,t-1}$ as used in the paper: the first row says the new position is the old position, plus the old velocity times an effective elapsed time $\tau(1-e^{-\Delta t/\tau})$, which approaches $\Delta t$ when $\Delta t \ll \tau$ (short steps relative to the memory: behaves like ordinary “position += velocity $\times$ time”) and saturates at $\tau$ when $\Delta t \gg \tau$ (long steps: the velocity has long since reverted to zero in between, so it contributes a bounded amount no matter how much additional time passes). The second row says the velocity itself just decays exponentially toward zero between updates, before the next noise term $d_t$ perturbs it again.

The process noise $Q_{d_t}$

The transition matrix alone would make $x_t$ decay deterministically to zero, which is clearly not the intent, the whole point is that $v_t$ keeps getting randomly perturbed. That randomness is the process noise $d_t \sim \mathcal{N}(0, Q_{d_t})$, and its covariance also follows directly from the OU process, via

$$ Q_{d_t} = \int_{t-1}^{t} \frac{2\sigma_v^2}{\tau} \left(e^{F(t-\zeta)}\right)^T G^T G\, e^{F(t-\zeta)}\, d\zeta. $$

Since $G=(0,1)^T$, $e^{F\Delta s}G$ is just the second column of the matrix computed above, $\left(\tau(1-e^{-\Delta s/\tau}),\, e^{-\Delta s/\tau}\right)^T$, so $G^Te^{F\Delta s\,T}e^{F\Delta s}G$ is the squared norm of that column. Writing $u = e^{-\Delta s/\tau}$ and substituting $\Delta s$ from $0$ to $\Delta t$, the velocity-velocity entry is the simplest to check by hand:

$$ q_{22} = \int_0^{\Delta t} \frac{2}{\tau} e^{-2\Delta s/\tau}\, d\Delta s = \frac{2}{\tau}\cdot\frac{\tau}{2}\left(1-e^{-2\Delta t/\tau}\right) = 1-e^{-2\Delta t/\tau}, $$

which matches the paper’s $q_{22}$ exactly (with $\sigma_v^2$ factored out front). The position-velocity term follows the same way:

$$ q_{21} = \int_0^{\Delta t} \frac{2}{\tau}\,\tau(1-u)\,u\, d\Delta s = 2\left[\tau(1-e^{-\Delta t/\tau}) - \tfrac{\tau}{2}(1-e^{-2\Delta t/\tau})\right] = \tau\left(1-e^{-\Delta t/\tau}\right)^2, $$

and expanding the square reproduces the paper’s $q_{21}=2\tau\left[-e^{-\Delta t/\tau}+\tfrac{1}{2}(1+e^{-2\Delta t/\tau})\right]$ term for term. The position-position entry $q_{11}$ is the same integral with $(1-u)^2$ in place of $u(1-u)$, messier to expand but mechanically identical, and it reproduces the paper’s $q_{11}$ the same way.

Simulating the smoothness constraint

Because $\Phi_{t,t-1}$ and $Q_{d_t}$ are now fully explicit, generating a synthetic IS trajectory is just running the discrete recursion $x_t = \Phi_{t,t-1}x_{t-1} + d_t$ forward.

import numpy as np

def simulate(sigma_v, tau, n_epochs=300, dt=1.0, rng=None):
    rng = rng or np.random.default_rng()
    Phi = np.array([[1.0, tau * (1 - np.exp(-dt / tau))],
                    [0.0, np.exp(-dt / tau)]])

    e1, e2 = np.exp(-dt / tau), np.exp(-2 * dt / tau)
    q11 = 2 * tau * (dt - 1.5 * tau + 2 * tau * e1 - 0.5 * tau * e2)
    q21 = 2 * tau * (-e1 + 0.5 * (1 + e2))
    q22 = 1 - e2
    Qd = sigma_v**2 * np.array([[q11, q21], [q21, q22]])
    L = np.linalg.cholesky(Qd)

    x = np.zeros((n_epochs, 2))              # columns: position, velocity
    for k in range(1, n_epochs):
        x[k] = Phi @ x[k - 1] + L @ rng.standard_normal(2)
    return x

Running this for 25 realizations at three settings of $(\sigma_v, \tau)$ shows what each parameter actually controls:

Comparing the first two panels (same $\tau=150$ days, $\sigma_v$ raised from 1 to 5 mm/yr) shows what $\sigma_v$ does: the paths keep the same qualitative smoothness, but the typical size of the excursions grows. Comparing the last two panels (same $\sigma_v=5$, $\tau$ dropped from 150 to 20 days) isolates what $\tau$ does: the paths become visibly rougher, more short-wavelength wiggling, and the overall spread after 300 days is actually smaller, because a short memory means velocity reverts to zero before a deviation has time to accumulate into a large displacement.

What this buys, compared to the first arc

In the first arc, “does this point have a changing behavior” was a yes/no question, answered by testing a library of discrete alternative hypotheses (a jump here, a seasonal term there) against a fixed null model. Here there is no library and no discrete test: the state is always free to drift, continuously, at a rate you control through $(\sigma_v, \tau)$ rather than through a menu of candidate shapes. Tightly constrained smoothness (small $\sigma_v$, large $\tau$) behaves almost like the old constant-velocity model; loosely constrained smoothness lets the estimate track real changes in behavior quickly, at the cost of also tracking noise more eagerly. Choosing $(\sigma_v, \tau)$ well is thus doing, informally, the same job that choosing which columns to test was doing in the first arc, just continuously instead of discretely.

What’s still missing is how any of this connects to the actual, wrapped phase observation arriving at each epoch, since so far this section has only described how the unobserved state evolves. That’s the subject of the next post.
